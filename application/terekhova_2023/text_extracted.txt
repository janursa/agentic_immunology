SUMMARY
Extensive, large-scale single-cell proﬁling of healthy human blood at different ages is one of the critical
pending tasks required to establish a framework for the systematic understanding of human aging. Here, us-
ing single-cell RNA/T cell receptor (TCR)/BCR-seq with protein feature barcoding, we proﬁled 317 samples
from 166 healthy individuals aged 25–85 years old. From this, we generated a dataset from/C242 million cells
that described 55 subpopulations of blood immune cells. Twelve subpopulations changed with age,
including the accumulation ofGZMK
+CD8+ T cells andHLA-DR+CD4+ T cells. In contrast to other T cell mem-
ory subsets, transcriptionally distinctNKG2C+GZMB/C0CD8+ T cells counterintuitively decreased with age.
Furthermore, we found a concerted age-associated increase in type 2/interleukin (IL)4-expressing memory
subpopulations across CD4
+ and CD8+ T cell compartments (CCR4+CD8+ Tcm and Th2 CD4+ Tmem), sug-
gesting a systematic functional shift in immune homeostasis with age. Our work provides novel insights
into healthy human aging and a comprehensive annotated resource.
INTRODUCTION
The human immune system changes substantially during aging,
even in the absence of disease. Understanding age-associated
changes in human PBMCs has served as the frontier of human
aging research. Various aspects of human blood undergo age-
associated changes, typically seen via cytometry-based ap-
proaches.
1–3 Several studies reporting small-scale dissection
of human aging using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)
typically focus on speciﬁc compartments such as natural killer
(NK) cells or CD8 + T cells. 1,4–10 These scRNA-seq studies pro-
vide comparisons of young vs. older cohorts encompassing
8–10 individuals, yielding data for /C24100,000 cells. Given that
age-associated changes are often gradual in nature, scRNA-
seq proﬁling of larger cohorts sampled across the whole range
of ages is required to dissect the complexity of immune aging
and achieve substantial statistical power. Furthermore, a robust
deﬁnition of rare immune cell subpopulations and identiﬁcation
of how they are altered by aging also require large-scale data
generation. Finally, transcriptional data require cytometry-based
validation as the gold-standard technique to verify the observed
subpopulations and their (age-related) proportional changes,
which has been largely missing, to date, in published studies.
Altogether, the ﬁeld of human immune aging would beneﬁt
from a large-scale scRNA-seq reference dataset that provides
a comprehensive description and validation of the key age-asso-
ciated changes in healthy human blood. Here, we performed an
extensive proﬁling on 317 PBMC samples from 166 healthy indi-
viduals (men and women) aged between 25 and 85 years old. All
samples were analyzed using scRNA-seq and feature barcoding
(FB), single-cell T cell receptor (TCR), and BCR sequencing,
which resulted in a dataset, encompassing approximately
2 million cells with 55 biologically signiﬁcant subpopulations. A
large scale of the data allowed us to ﬁnd 12 subpopulations
that change with age, including a relatively low-abundant
NKG2C
+GZMB/C0CD8+ memory T cell subset. We observe age-
related accumulation of T helper (Th)2-like CD4 + memory cells
and CCR4+ CD8+ central memory T cells, the latter of which
are enriched in type 2 immune signature. This indicates func-
tional bias in age-associated remodeling of immune cells in

RESULTS
Overall study design and immune cell analysis
We focused on understanding the changes in PBMC composi-
tion during healthy aging ( Figure 1 A). Accordingly, all donors
with inﬂammatory conditions or cancer were excluded from the
study. We also excluded donors based on lifestyle variables,
such as obesity or smoking, known to affect the immune system.
Blood was always collected in the morning, and participants
were required to fast prior to collection. In parallel with PBMC
collections, we performed extensive clinical blood panel tests
to evaluate the health status of individuals. These showed no
acute inﬂammation (hs-CRP), tissue infarction (LDH), myocardial
injury (creatine kinase), or lipid imbalance (cholesterol and tri-
glycerides) together with no changes in the adrenal system
(cortisol) in our cohort. The donors were metabolically healthy
as insulin and glucose levels were all within the healthy range.
Liver and kidney functions were also normal based on ALT,
AST, total bilirubin, total protein, albumin, creatinine, and BUN
(Figures 1 B, S1A, and S1B). Among these, albumin decreased
and BUN increased with age (within the healthy range) demon-
strating age-associated effects on renal systems consistent
with previous reports.
14,15 Well-described age-associated
changes were seen in red blood cell count and hemoglobin, 16
which all decrease with age. Similarly, classical hormonal signa-
tures of aging were observed—a decline in DHEA-S 17 and an in-
crease in TSH. 18 Taken together, our exclusion criteria and clin-
ical panel tests conﬁrm that our donors were of generally good
health and demonstrated typical biochemical features of aging.
317 samples were collected over the course of 3 years and
then proﬁled using scTCR/scBCR/scRNA-seq with 20 antibody
features ( Figures 1 A and S1C; Tables S1 and S2). The libraries
were constructed in 14 batches, and count data were harmo-
nized
19 across individual batches ( Figures S1 D and S1E;
Table S3)( STAR Methods). To deﬁne individual immune cell sub-
populations, we utilized a hierarchical approach: the ﬁrst major
immune subsets were deﬁned (e.g., CD4 + and CD8 + T, NK, B,
and myeloid cells); then, these major subsets were further sub-
clustered. Subclustering resolution of major immune subtypes
was chosen such that it generally matched transcriptional signa-
tures of biologically well-deﬁned subpopulations (e.g., CD4
+
Th2, Th1/Th17, and Th17 cell signatures from Monaco et al. 20
or central and effector memory subtypes of CD8 + T cells from
Azimuth human PBMC reference 21)( STAR Methods ), resulting
in a total of 55 distinct immune PBMC subpopulations.
To assess the signiﬁcance of age-associated changes, we uti-
lized two approaches. First, samples coming from the ﬁrst visits
were split into 5 age groups with 10-year-long intervals (25–34,
35–44, 45–54, 55–64, and 65+ years old), resulting in 22–47 sam-
ples per age group, and then statistical comparison of subpop-
ulation frequencies between the groups was carried out using
Kruskal-Wallis test. Second, linear regression analysis against
the donor’s ages was performed as a conﬁrmatory evaluation.
Age- and sex-speciﬁc abundance of clusters and differential
expression within the clusters were then evaluated to validate
our ﬁndings using multidimensional cytometry with a custom-
designed panel of 30 markers ( Table S4 ).
After quality control ﬁltering ( STAR Methods ), our dataset
comprised /C242 million cells ( Figure 1 C). On average, we proﬁled
6,000 cells per sample, detecting 1,300 genes per cell
(Figures S1F and S1G). scRNA-seq data readily identiﬁed major
PBMC subpopulations: CD4
+, conventional CD8 +, MAIT CD8 +,
DN and gd T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, NK cells, and progenitor
subpopulations (Figures 1C and S1H; Table S5). Consistent with
previous reports, overall CD4 + T cell proportions did not change
with age, and conventional (non-MAIT) CD8 + T cells decreased
with age, 7,22,23 resulting in the well-recognized effect of an
age-associated increase in the CD4 +/CD8+ T cell ratio 24–26
(Figures 1 D, 1E, S1I–S1K, and S2A–S2C; Table S6 ). MAIT cells
declined after /C2455 years of age ( Figures 1 D and S1I–S1K;
Table S6). We next zoomed in on each of these subsets to inter-
rogate the ﬁne structure of the cell subpopulations and probe
their age-dependent remodeling.
Myeloid cells remain relatively static throughout aging
We identiﬁed 4 major myeloid subpopulations in our dataset—
classical (CD14 +CD16/C0) and non-classical (CD16 +) monocytes,
pDCs, and CD1C-expressing cDCs ( Figures S2 D and S2E;
Table S5 ). No age-associated proportional or transcriptional
remodeling was observed in myeloid subpopulations
(Figures S2 F–S2I; Tables S6 and S7), which is consistent with
previous work on healthy aging in monocytes.
27 We next per-
formed cell-cell communication analysis, where age-related dif-
ferences were observed in SEMA4D signaling within myeloid and
T cells and FLT3 signaling within progenitor and T cells ( Fig-
ure S2 J). Sema4D on T cells was shown to promote the activa-
tion and maturation of DCs,
28 whereas Flt3 ligand was involved
in the development of hematopoietic precursors 29; however,
the speciﬁc roles of these pathways are still being investigated.

CD4+ cells: Subpopulation structure and age-associated
changes
CD4+ T cells comprised 901,152 cells, which allowed us to
deeply interrogate subpopulation structures and age-dependent
changes within the subpopulations ( Figures 2 A–2D and S2K–
S2N). Broadly, the CD4
+ T cell subpopulations formed four major
groups: naive cells (CD45RA +CCR7+) (clusters 0–1), helper
memory cells (CD45RO +) (clusters 2–9), cytotoxic CD4 + T cells
(PRF1+GNLY+) (clusters 10–12), and regulatory cells ( FOXP3+)
(clusters 13–15).
In accord with the previous studies, naive CD4 + T cells (cluster
0) only slightly decreased with age without reaching statistical
signiﬁcance30–33 (Figures 2D, S2M, and S2N; Table S6), attribut-
able to the ability of naive CD4 + T cells to maintain their numbers
efﬁciently through homeostatic proliferation. 34 Memory helper
CD4+ T cells separated into 8 clusters ( Figures 2 B and S3A;
Table S5 ). Most of these clusters could be deﬁned by a distinct
pattern of chemokine receptor expression ( CCR4, CCR6,
CXCR5, and CXCR3 genes, Figure S3 B) and were validated
against the previously published proﬁles of the sorted memory
subpopulations described in Monaco et al. 20 and Ho¨ llbacher
et al. 35 (Figure S3 C). Follicular memory Tfh cells (cluster 2)
were identiﬁed based on the strong expression of CXCR526
(Figures 2 B, S3B, and S3D). Th1 memory cells (cluster 3) were
characterized by the presence of CXCR3 expression while lack-
ing the expressions of CCR6 and KLRB1 (Figures 2 B, S3B, and
S3D). The CCR6+CXCR3+ subpopulation was deﬁned as
Th1/Th17 memory cells 20 (cluster 4): this population selectively
expressed KLRB1 (Figures 2 B, S3B, and S3D). GZMK was
expressed in not only, to a greater extent, Th1 cells but also
Th1/Th17 cells ( Figures 2 B and S3D). The subpopulation of
CCR6
+CXCR3/C0 was identiﬁed as Th17 memory cells 20 (cluster
5) and expressed the lineage-speciﬁc transcriptional factor
RORC along with AHR (Figures 2 B, S3B, and S3D). In addition,
clustering deﬁned a distinct subpopulation that co-expressed
CCR4, CCR6, and CCR10 (Figures 2 B and S3B) and, therefore,
was deﬁned as the Th22 memory population (cluster 6) based on
Trifari et al.
36 This population showed enrichment of Th22 signa-
tures deﬁned by Ho ¨ llbacher et al. 35 (Figure S3 C). Th2 memory
cells (cluster 7) were deﬁned as the CCR4+CCR6/C0 population
and expressed transcription factor GATA320 (Figures 2 B and
S3B). We also identiﬁed another memory cluster that was
marked with elevated transcriptional levels of HLA-DR, therefore
referred to as HLA-DR+ CD4+ Tmem cells (cluster 8) ( Figures 2B
and S3D). Finally, we identiﬁed a cluster of CD4 + memory T cells
that was enriched with CTLA-4, PDCD1, LAG3, and HAVCR2
(TIM-3) (Figures 2B, S3B, and S3D), which we tentatively termed
exhausted-like CD4 + T cells (cluster 9). This cluster also ex-
pressed GZMK at higher levels ( Figures 2 B and S3D). Among
helper memory subpopulations, only Th2 and HLA-DR+ Tmem
cells substantially accumulated with age ( Figures 2 D and S2L–
S2N; Table S6 ).
Three cytotoxic CD4 + T cell subpopulations were very
distinct from the other cells and expressed classical cytotox-
icity markers granulysin ( GNLY) and perforin ( PRF1), and tran-
scription factors EOMES and TBX21 (encoding T-BET). The
observed populations exhibited distinct patterns of GZMK
and GZMB expressions ( Figures 2 Ba n d S3D). GZMK and
GZMB coexpressing cytotoxic CD4 + T cells were referred to
as terminal effector CD4 + TTE (cluster 10) based on the absence
of expressions of both CCR7 and CD45RA 37 (Figures 2 Ba n d
S3D). The GZMB-expressing cytotoxic CD4+ T cells, which
exhibited elevated CD45RA surface protein signal (based on
single-cell Feature Barcode data) ( Figures 2 Ba n d S3D)
were tentatively referred to as CD4
+ terminally differentiated
CD45RA+ memory (Temra) cells 37 (cluster 11). The third cyto-
toxic cluster is comprised of GZMB-expressing cytotoxic
regulatory T cells (Tregs) ( FOXP3+CD25+IL7Rlow) (cluster 12)
(Figures 2 B, S3D, and S3E). None of the cytotoxic CD4 + Tc e l l
subpopulations changed appreciably with age ( Figures 2 D,
S2M, and S2N; Table S6 ).
Apart from cytotoxic GZMB+PRF1+GNLY+ Tregs, we de-
tected 3 other distinct CD4 + Treg subsets in PBMCs. All
Treg clusters exhibited co-expression of FOXP3 and high
levels of CD25 antibody, whereas they demonstrated low
expression of IL7R (CD127) ( Figures 2 B, S3D, and S3E). These
clusters were also enriched in the Monaco et al. 20 Treg bulk
signature ( Figure S3 C). Those included naive Treg cells
(CD45RA+CCR7+SELL+LEF1+TCF7+)( c l u s t e r1 3 ) ,m e m o r y
Treg cells ( IL2RA+CCR4+CTLA-4+ and HLA-DR+)38 (cluster
14), and a distinct subset of memory KLRB1+RORC+ Treg
cells (cluster 15), which also expressed CCR4 and CTLA-4
but were lacking HLA-DR and IKZF2 expressions
(Figures 2 Ba n d S3D). With age, proportions of naive Treg

With respect to TCR repertoire, cytotoxic CD4 + Temra and

CD4+ T cells: Th2 andHLA-DR+ Tmem accumulation and
naive cells remodeling
To validate our ﬁndings, we devised a 30-color ﬂow cytometry
panel ( Table S4 ) guided by the results from scRNA-seq. We
analyzed PBMCs from 26 individuals (13 young vs. 13 old, see
Figure 3 A; Table S8 ) focusing on subpopulations emerging
from our scRNA-seq analysis. Consistent with scRNA-seq
data, we indeed observed that naive CD4
+ T cells did not change
with age ( Figures 3B and 3C). Differential gene expression anal-
ysis of CD4 + T cells between young vs. old subgroups demon-
strated strong evidence of age-associated transcriptional re-
modeling in naive cells ( Figure 3 D; Table S7 ). Enrichment in
Hallmark gene set collection
39 highlighted two major changes
in naive cells with age: metabolic modiﬁcations and active cyto-
kine signaling ( Figures 3E–3G). Interleukin (IL)2-STAT5 signaling
was one of the strongest elevated pathways, including its key
members IL2RA and IL2RB (Figures 3 G–3I). Accordingly, naive
CD4
+ T cells from old individuals increased surface expression
of CD25 to a greater extent than naive cells from young donors
(Figure 3J).40 The increase in IL2-STAT5 signaling axis is consis-
tent with enhanced homeostatic proliferation of naive CD4 +
T cells in aged humans, which would rely on minute quantities
of circulating IL2. 30,41,42 To rule out potential association be-
tween age-related increase in CD25 surface expression and acti-
vation of naive cells, we evaluated enrichment of classical T cell
activation signatures in the aged naive CD4
+ T cells ( Figure S3H)
and found no positive enrichment. Proportions of naive Treg cells
decreased with age, whereas the overall total fraction of Treg
cells did not change ( Figures S3 I–S3K) due to compensation
by a corresponding increase in memory Treg cell proportions
(Figure S3 L). This suggests that regulatory naive T cells might
be less prone to homeostatic proliferation compared with con-
ventional CD4
+ naive cells, which do not change with age
despite decreased thymic output. 41
Considering age-associated changes in helper memory sub-
populations, we devised a gating strategy to obtain HLA-DR+
memory CD4 + Th cells and memory Th2 CD4 + T cells
(Figures 3 K, 3L, and 3N). In both cases, we directly validated
scRNA-seq ﬁndings using cytometry data ( Figures 3 M and
3O). In terms of magnitude, CD4 + Th2 memory cells demon-
strated the biggest increase with age, suggesting that the func-
tionality of the memory helper compartment might be undergo-
ing a corresponding immunological bias toward type 2
immunity.
40 Both HLA-DR+ memory and Th2 cells not only
increased in proportion but also showed an increase in clonality
with age, further pointing toward their potential functional signif-
icance ( Figure S3 F).
Th2 and HLA-DR+ memory cells together with Tfh, Th1/Th17,
and naive Treg cells were enriched in various pathways related to
tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) in elderly individuals ( Figure S3 M)
and Rho GTPase cycle in young. 43 These ﬁndings suggest a
mitochondrial dysfunction in T cells, resulting in the generation
of abundant reactive oxygen species and contributing to inﬂam-
maging.44 Taken together, our results highlight the accumulation
of Th2 and HLA-DR+ CD4 T cells and naive cells remodeling as
key hallmarks of healthy aging in circulating CD4 T cells.

DISCUSSION
Here, we present a comprehensive dissection of the age-associ-
ated changes in healthy human blood. Proﬁling 317 samples al-
lowed us to achieve a very high depth of analysis and robustly
identify 55 distinct subpopulations, including a novel subpopula-
tion of NKG2C
+GZMB/C0CD8+ memory T cells that exhibit unusual
decreases with age. Similar to previous reports, we did not
observe sex-speciﬁc differences within PBMC subpopulations
in any age group
4 (STAR Methods). Additionally, no BMI-speciﬁc
difference (<25 vs. 25–30) was observed in age-related behavior
of individual subpopulations ( STAR Methods ). We ﬁnd major
age-associated remodeling in the memory CD4 + T cell compart-
ment characterized by the increase of Th2 memory cells, HLA-
DR+ memory CD4+ T cells, and memory Treg cells accompanied
by a strong decrease in naive Treg cells. The age-associated
behavior of HLA-DR+ CD4+ Tmem cells has not been previously
reported in the literature, and we validated our observation using
spectral cytometry. Dissection of the CD8
+ T cell compartment
demonstrated an increase in CCR4+ CD8+ Tcm cells that share
type 2 immunity signatures with Th2 CD4 + memory cells and
are highly correlated across our cohort. These CD8 + T cells ex-
press IL4 transcript and likely are a source population behind
the ability of human CD8 + PBMC to produce IL-4. 52,53 This sug-
gests the intriguing possibility that remodeling of immune ho-
meostasis during healthy aging is associated with increased
type 2 immunity hallmarks in both CD4
+ and CD8 + compart-
ments. Furthermore, CCR4+ CD8+ Tcm cells accumulated at a
higher pace than CCR4/C0 CD8+ Tcm cells, resulting in CCR4+/
CCR4/C0 CD8+ Tcm ratio as a robust correlate of age. We also
observed previously reported an accumulation of GZMK+
effector memory subpopulation. 7,22
The overall prevalence of asthma and allergic asthma does not
change with age, 51,61 together with such conditions as eczema
and seasonal and food allergies. 62 However, non-allergic
asthma cases increase with age. 61 Our measurement of type 2
cytokines revealed no age difference for baseline levels, but
upon stimulation, we observed their increase in CD8 + T cells
with age.52,53 This suggested a potential increase in predisposi-
tion with age to certain conditions that may not manifest in a sta-
ble state but can be triggered by various stimuli. An example of
such a condition could be idiopathic pulmonary ﬁbrosis, which
predominantly affects individuals between 50 and 70 years
old.
63 Patients with pulmonary ﬁbrosis display a Th1/Th2 imbal-
ance, with a prevalence of Th2 cells in bronchoalveolar lavage
ﬂuid, and IL-4 has been implicated in increasing the severity of
ﬁbrosis.64
The KLRC2+ (NKG2C+GZMB/C0) CD8 + Tmem subset was a
relatively small but transcriptionally distinct subpopulation of
memory cells that surprisingly decreased with age. This subset
has never been explicitly discussed in the literature to date; how-
ever, we were able to match this signature to the transcriptional
cluster in the published scRNA-seq data,
21,50 indicating that this
population is a robust and reproducible group of cells. Accord-
ingly, we designed a cytometry-based approach to validate
these cells. We explicitly identiﬁed these cells as KLRC2
+
(NKG2C+ GZMB/C0) CD8 + Tmem cells and conﬁrmed their age-
associated decline.
Antigen receptor sequencing allowed us to gain unique in-
sights into age-associated changes of the individual subpopu-
lations. We found that in CD4
+ T cells, cytotoxic T cells were
generally more clonal than other subpopulations, with Temra
CD4+ T cells increasing in clonality. We also observed that
the Th1/Th17 subpopulation demonstrated an age-associated
increase in clonality. In CD8 + Tc e l l s ,GZMK+ Tem and CCR4/C0
Tcm cells strongly increased clonality with age, whereas
CCR4+ Tcm cells did not change their clonality. Remarkably,
the analysis of TCR sharing indicated that more than half of
effector memory CD8 + T cells shared clonotypes between
each other and other memory populations.
Our data yielded unique biological insights into age-associ-
ated changes and also provided a resource of exceptional depth
in both raw data and browsable form (Synapse under accession

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
Human samples
The Washington University in St. Louis School of Medicine Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the ABF300 study for
the collection of blood samples from healthy participants (IRB-201804084). All participants provided written informed consent.
Caucasian, non-obese (BMI under 30) males and females were enrolled in the study. Participants were given a screening question-
naire to establish their health status. Non-smokers without a history of cancer, chronic inﬂammatory conditions (arthritis, Crohn’s
disease, colitis, dermatitis, ﬁbromyalgia, or lupus), or blood-borne infections (HIV, hepatitis B, and C) were included in the study. Sub-
jects who reported cold or ﬂu symptoms in the prior month were excluded. Peripheral blood ( /C24100 ml) was collected into sodium-

Human plasma and PBMC isolation
Plasma was isolated from the whole blood by centrifugation at 500 3 g for 30 min at room temperature. The top plasma layer was
recovered, and aliquots were stored at –80
/C14C. The remaining whole blood was used for PBMC isolation by density gradient centri-
fugation using Histopaque-1077 (Sigma, 10771) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Brieﬂy, the whole blood was diluted in
a 1:1 ratio with DPBS (Gipco, 14190136) with 2mM EDTA (Corning, 46-034-CI). The diluted blood was overlaid on Histopaque-1077
and centrifuged at 500 3 g for 30 min at room temperature without breaks. PBMC were isolated from diluted plasma - Histopaque
interface and washed twice in DPBS–2mM EDTA. PBMC were cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 freezing medium (Biolife Solutions,
210502) and stored at –80 /C14C.

Single-cell RNA-seq
Prior to scRNA-seq assays, cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed by serial dilutions with an equal volume of Wash Buffer [1% BSA in
PBS] starting with 0.5 mL. Once the volume was equal to 12 mL, the samples were centrifuged at 400 x g for 5 min at 4 C. Cells were
washed with an additional 1 mL Wash Buffer and pelleted at 400 x g for 5 min at 4 C.
Cell pellets were resuspended in 50 uL Wash Buffer. 5 uL Human TruStain FcX was added and cells were incubated 10 mins at 4 C. 10
uL of a cocktail of BioLegend TotalSeq-C antibodies, along with 0.1 ug of a TotalSeq-C Hashtag antibody. Samples were incubated at 4 C
for 30 mins. All antibody cocktails were spun at 14,000 x g for 10 mins at 4 C prior to addition to cells to remove antibody aggregates.
Three stringent washes were performed after antibody labeling with Wash Buffer. Cells were counted and assessed for viability.
Cells were combined in equal pools of up to six samples per pool. Pooled cell samples were washed a ﬁnal time with 2mL 1 Wash
Buffer, ﬁltered through a 40 micron FloMi ﬁlter, and resuspended in 200 uL Wash Buffer. Pooled cells were counted and assessed for
viability, and the ﬁnal suspension was brought to /C243200 cells/uL.
Pools of cells labeled with TotalSeq-C hashtag antibodies were subjected to droplet-based massively parallel single-cell RNA
sequencing using Chromium Single Cell 5
0v2 Reagent Kit as per manufacturer’s instructions (10x Genomics). Brieﬂy, cell suspen-
sions were loaded at 3,200 cells/uL, adding 30.9 uL of cells plus 12.3 uL of Nuclease Free Water to the Reverse Transcription Master
Mix. This resulted in loading a total of /C2498,880 cells per GEM well. Each pool was loaded across two GEM wells.
The 10x Chromium Controller generated GEM droplets, where each cell was labeled with a speciﬁc barcode, and each transcript
labeled with a unique molecular identiﬁer (UMI) during reverse transcription. The barcoded cDNA was isolated via a Dynabeads My-
One SILANE bead cleanup mixture. The cDNA was ampliﬁed by PCR and puriﬁed via SPRI bead cleanup.
For gene expression libraries, 50 ng of ampliﬁed cDNA was used for library preparation, consisting of fragmentation, end repair,
A-tailing, adaptor ligation and sample index PCR as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq S4
(300 cycles) ﬂow cell, targeting 25,000 read pairs per cell.
For human TCR repertoire libraries, 2 uL of ampliﬁed cDNA underwent two rounds of Target Enrichment using nested primer pairs
speciﬁc for human TCR constant regions. For BCR repertoire libraries, 5 uL of ampliﬁed cDNA was used as input to the Target Enrich-
ment PCR reactions, due to the lower level of anticipated B cells in the PBMC samples. 50 ng of the target enrichment PCR product
was used for library preparation, consisting of fragmentation, end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation and sample index PCR as per the
manufacturer’s instructions. Feature Barcode libraries were generated from the captured oligo tags from TotalSeq-C antibodies by
completing a sample index PCR reaction per the manufacturer’s instructions. Enriched and Feature Barcode libraries were
sequenced on a NovaSeq S4 (300 cycles) ﬂow cell, targeting 5,000 read pairs per cell.
Demultiplexing of pooled single-cell RNA-seq
Samples arrangement
Total 317 samples were splitted into 14 batches. For each batch, 4 pools of 6 samples were taken, and for each pool there were 2
independent technical replicates, resulting in 8 sequencing outputs. One batch contained only one pool and were divided into 3 tech-
nical replicates. Each pool contained samples coming from different individuals. In overall there were 107 sequencing outputs com-
ing from 317 samples. Each sequencing output was then independently demultiplexed in the following way.
Demultiplexing pipeline
To demultiplex individual technical replicate, we used a combination of hashtag-based (barcoded antibodies) and genotype-based
approaches. In short, output from hashtag-based demultiplexing algorithm Seurat
21 v4.0.5 HTODemux was used to split BAM ﬁles
for an individual sample with the subset-bam function (10x Genomics) v1.1.0. Next, within the technical replicate we applied the
minimap266 v2.17-r941 and freebayes67 v1.3.2 tools to create VCF ﬁles enriched in individual sample variants. Next, corresponding
VCF ﬁles were used as an input for souporcell pipeline68 v2.5 to perform genotype-based demultiplexing. Pipeline script for demul-
tiplexing are publicly available (see data and code availability).
This approach allowed to improve the number of demultiplexed cells compared to using only one of these demultiplexing methods.
The quality of demultiplexing was assessed based on similarity of results between two technical replicates containing the same sam-
ples. In the case when one of the demultiplexing approaches did not yield conclusive results, another approach was independently
applied for demultiplexing. In overall, 2 out of 107 sequencing outputs failed in genotype-based demultiplexing and 6 out of 107
sequencing outputs failed in HTO-based demultiplexing.

Single-cell multi-omic analysis
Cell Ranger countpipeline v7.0.0 (available at 10x website) was applied for read alignment to reference genome GRCh38 (GENCODE
v32/Ensembl 98), quantiﬁcation of gene expression and surface proteins in individual technical replicates. Downstream single-cell
analysis was performed using Seurat package21 v4.0.5 within the R programming environment 82 v4.1.2.
Batch-level analysis
Technical replicates were merged into corresponding batches resulting in 14 datasets. Filtering thresholds were chosen based on
binary logarithm distribution of quality metrics. Cells with less than 9 log
2 (abs = 512) of the number of genes were ﬁltered out, as
well as cells with more than 2.6 log 2 (abs /C246) of percentage of mitochondrial counts. Moreover, we left only cells with more than
10 and less than 12.7 log 2 (abs = 1024 and 6654, respectively) of the number of UMIs. Cell doublets were determined using the
Scrublet package69 v0.2.3, as well as based on a high level of expression of more than one cell population-speciﬁc markers (genes
or proteins) and removed from individual datasets. Red blood cells and platelets were removed based on expression of speciﬁc gene
markers. Filtered matrices were normalized using a scaling factor of 10,000 and centered. Surface protein assays were normalized
across cells using centered log ratio transformation. Variable features were selected based on average feature expression between
0.0125 and 3, as well as dispersion higher than 0.5. Genes encoding alpha and beta TCR chains, as well as heavy and light
BCR chains were excluded from variable features. Two sources of unwanted variation, total number of counts and percentage of
counts belonging to mitochondrial genes, were regressed using a linear model. Batch integration was performed during subsequent
steps while analyzing individual populations and whole-dataset assembly. UMAP dimensional reduction and shared nearest
neighbor graph were calculated using 40 principal components. Graph-based clustering was performed on the reduced data using
resolution 0.2.
PBMC population-level analysis
Each batch dataset was split based on gene and surface protein expression into 4 subsets: T cells together with NK cells, Myeloid
cells, B cells and progenitor cells. Next, each subset was merged with a corresponding subset coming from other batches, resulting
in 4 separate datasets for major PBMC populations. Merging was performed with normalized matrices with subsequent scaling.
Variable features were selected based on average expression and dispersion with cut-offs to leave /C241,500 variable features. Genes
encoding alpha and beta TCR chains, as well as heavy and light BCR chains were excluded from variable features. Two sources of
unwanted variation, total number of counts and percentage of counts belonging to mitochondrial genes, were regressed using a
linear model. Within the datasets, batches were integrated using the harmony correction algorithm
19 v0.1.0. UMAP dimensional
reduction and shared nearest neighbor graph were calculated on harmony corrected PCA embeddings. Number of principal com-
ponents was selected based on the elbow plot. Number of neighbors for the graph was set to 15. Resolution parameter for graph-
based clustering was chosen for each dataset individually.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
For pairwise comparisons of cell-type-speciﬁc percentages between age groups only information from the ﬁrst visit was used. Com-
parisons were performed using post-hoc Dunn’s test ( dunnTest function from FSA package84 v0.8.30) after one-way Kruskal–Wallis
test with Bonferroni adjustment. Obtained p adjusted values were additionally adjusted with Bonferroni method by the number of
total subpopulations within each cell-type group.
Two-way factorial Scheirer–Ray–Hare test ( scheirerRayHare function from rcompanion package85 v2.3.25) with Bonferroni
adjustment was used to examine sex difference within cell-type-speciﬁc subpopulation percentages and the effect of sex on age-
associated changes. To examine potential BMI effect, patients were grouped based on BMI: < 25 and 25-30. Two-way factorial
Scheirer–Ray–Hare test with Bonferroni adjustment was used to examine BMI-group difference within cell-type-speciﬁc subpopu-
lation percentages and the effect of BMI-group on age-associated changes.
For other comparisons, the name and type of the statistical test are stated in the Figure legends.
All applied tests are nonparametric and thus no assumption is required on the data distribution.
