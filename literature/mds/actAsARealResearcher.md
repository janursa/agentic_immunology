# Act As a Real Researcher: A Suite of Benchmarks Evaluating Frontier LLMs and Agentic Harnesses in Research Lifecycle

## Abstract
As foundation models advance and agent scaffolding becomes increasingly sophisticated, agents have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in complex, long-horizon coding tasks and even autonomous experiment execution. Despite their evolution from research assistants into autonomous research agents, these systems still exhibit significant limitations in field sensitivity, research ethics, and nuanced scientific judgment. Consequently, frontier agents remain unable to fully replace human researchers. To bridge this gap, we conceptualize the AARR (Act As a Real Researcher) benchmark series. Unlike existing benchmarks that primarily assess macro-level execution capabilities, AARR focuses on whether agents can emulate the professionalism, thoroughness, and nuanced reasoning that characterize human researchers in granular research scenarios. In this work, we propose AARRI-Bench (Act As a Real Research Intern), the first benchmark in this series. We conduct extensive experiments across frontier models and agentic systems, revealing that even the best-performing configuration (Mini-SWE-Agent with Claude Opus 4.7) achieves only 68.3% success rate, frequently overlooking subtle yet critical details that are obvious to real human researchers. Our results indicate that developing researcher-like AI requires further exploration of research behavior, rather than merely complex scaffolding. Data released at https://github.com/AARR-bench/AARRI-bench.

## Methods
**AARRI-Bench overview.** AARRI-Bench is the first work in the AARR (Act As a Real Researcher) series, aiming to uncover the gaps between AI agents and real-world research interns. Its tasks span daily AI-research scenarios that are straightforward for human researchers but pose substantial challenges for autonomous agents. Tasks are systematically designed and manually crafted by researchers to reflect genuine pain points encountered in practice. Evaluation is built on the Harbor framework, which standardizes task format and provides a clean, containerized environment, enabling simultaneous evaluation of both the underlying model and the agent harness.

**Data taxonomy.** Tasks are categorized along two orthogonal dimensions:

- *Horizontal (task scenario categories)*: (1) **Context** — sensitivity to the broader academic/field context (e.g., identifying a paper's core contributions, assessing data validity, distinguishing genuine advances from reviewer-pandering work); (2) **Mindset** — academic self-awareness and decision-making autonomy, including the willingness to disagree with human instructions, form independent judgments, and recognize dead-end research directions; (3) **Hands-on** — execution-oriented technical proficiency (coding, experimental setup, data processing); (4) **Interaction** — effective use of tools and appropriate collaboration with human stakeholders across multi-turn workflows.
- *Vertical (agent scope taxonomy)*: (1) **S1-Adaptation** (32% of tasks) — following instructions, using standard tools, completing well-defined subtasks; (2) **S2-Integration** (28%) — integrating multiple components/tools for more complex goals; (3) **S3-Innovation** (27%) — making meaningful intellectual contributions with minimal guidance, identifying promising directions; (4) **S4-Open-ended** (13%) — tackling ambiguous problems requiring deep insight and methodological innovation.

**Task structure.** Each task is a Harbor-format directory containing: `instruction.md` (task instructions and expected behavior/goals), `task.toml` (metadata: category, creator, environment settings), `environment/` (Dockerfile defining the execution container), `solution/` (reference solution scripts, e.g. `solve.sh`), and `tests/` (verifier script, e.g. `test.sh`, producing reward outputs).

**Construction process.** All tasks were manually crafted by a diverse team of researchers (from senior PhD students to undergraduate interns) drawing on their own research pain points, through a three-stage process: (1) *Free Creation* — participants freely design tasks from personal pain points across the four horizontal categories; (2) *Guided Expansion* — task topics are aggregated and analyzed, with customized design directions assigned to each contributor to refine/expand initial designs; (3) *Consolidation* — all tasks are compiled, categorized vertically by agent scope, and duplicates are removed/refined. This yielded the final benchmark of 82 tasks organized along the two dimensions.

**Evaluation setup.** Evaluations use the Harbor framework across 16 representative harness–model combinations.
- *Agent harnesses*: Claude Code, Hermes Agent (Nous Research), and the open-source Mini-SWE-Agent.
- *Models*: closed-source — Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.3 Codex, Qwen 3.6 Plus; open-source — MiniMax-M2.7, Kimi K2.6, DeepSeek-V4-Flash. APIs sourced from official providers and OpenRouter (no third-party relay services).
- *Environment*: runs performed on cloud platforms recommended by Harbor's official documentation (Daytona, Modal) to ensure reproducibility and minimize local-machine variance.
- *Metrics*: two granularities — (1) **Classic 0/1 reward**, following SWE-bench/Terminal-bench style scoring, using final task completion as the sole criterion (used for headline/overall performance); (2) **Fine-grained unit tests**, where each task's verifier comprises multiple manually crafted unit tests, used for case studies and in-depth analysis rather than headline scoring.

## Results
**Main results.** The highest-performing configuration overall is Mini-SWE-Agent + Claude-Opus-4.7, achieving 68.3% success rate — outperforming more complex, feature-rich harnesses such as Hermes Agent (64.6%) and Claude Code (62.2%) paired with the same model. This suggests complex agent scaffolding is not a prerequisite for superior performance; minimalist agent architectures with low-level primitives can outperform feature-heavy designs, likely by minimizing cognitive overhead for frontier models. Across all setups, performance drops significantly with lower-tier models, confirming the underlying model's intrinsic reasoning capability remains the primary bottleneck for autonomous research tasks. Per-category overall scores (Classic 0/1 reward) range roughly from 51.3% (Claude Code + Kimi-K2.6) to 68.3% (Mini-SWE-Agent + Claude-Opus-4.7); Hermes Agent + Claude-Opus-4.7 scored 64.6%, and Claude Code + Claude-Opus-4.7 scored 62.2%, with Mindset-category scores for these top configurations reaching 76.9%.

**Model scaling and harness synergy.** Relative to the overall baseline minimum score of 51.3% (Claude Code + Kimi K2.6), lower-tier models (e.g., MiniMax-M2.7) yield closely clustered performance across all harnesses (56.1%–58.1%), but transitioning to a frontier model (Claude-Opus-4.7) produces highly disparate scaling behavior: the minimalist Mini-SWE-Agent gains +11.5% success rate with Claude-Opus-4.7, while the more structured Claude Code gains only +6.1%. This implies rigid, over-engineered execution harnesses can restrict the scaling potential of highly intelligent models, whereas minimalist harnesses give advanced models the flexibility to navigate complex scientific environments.

**Execution efficiency and trajectory statistics.** Distinct behavioral traits emerge by harness design: (1) Claude Code shows wide, long-tailed step distributions (max steps 131 with MiniMax-M2.7, 95 with Qwen-3.6-Plus), indicating its interactive feedback loop is prone to runaway/redundant execution when the model struggles; (2) Hermes Agent shows highly condensed step distributions with low average step counts (µ = 8.4 and 9.0), reflecting its specialized toolset and structured constraints enforcing optimized, direct trajectories; (3) Mini-SWE-Agent occupies a middle ground with a stable distribution robust to the underlying model (µ = 15.4 for MiniMax-M2.7, 15.9 for Qwen-3.6-Plus), indicating a minimalist interface maintains a predictable execution footprint, avoiding both catastrophic looping and premature termination.

**Case studies.**
- *Task 26fb63 (Identifying Fabricated Data during Review)*: Agents review a manuscript whose accompanying dataset contains fabricated data (all trailing decimal digits identical) — a pattern highly obvious to a human reader — and are instructed to assign a low score if serious issues are found. This proved an exceptional challenge: most agents overlooked the numerical anomaly, instead flagging minor reproducibility issues. Only Claude Code + Claude Opus 4.7 successfully detected the fabricated-data pattern; Hermes Agent + Claude Opus 4.7 produced a thorough but ultimately unsuccessful review that missed this critical point.
- *Task 429504 (Avoiding Memory Confusion during Multi-round Exploration)*: Tests whether an agent remembers and avoids re-proposing previously rejected directions, even when reworded. Hermes Agent + Claude Opus 4.7 recognized a keyword-filtering issue at step 8 but had already submitted the same direction under two different phrasings, both mapped to the same rejected category, causing duplicate categories and test failure. Mini-SWE-Agent + Claude Opus 4.7 captured category boundaries more accurately, generating a broader set of genuinely distinct novel categories and avoiding repeated rejected categories.

## Conclusions
In this work, we conceptualize the AARR benchmark series for evaluating LLM agents in authentic research scenarios. Specifically, we introduce AARRI-Bench, the inaugural benchmark in this series, and conduct extensive experiments across frontier models and agent harnesses. Our results show that despite recent advances in long-horizon agent capabilities, current systems still struggle with many subtle yet important details in real research workflows that remain straightforward for human researchers. We hope these findings can provide insights for future design, training, and evaluation of agentic AI systems.
