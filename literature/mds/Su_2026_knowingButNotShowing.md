# Knowing but Not Showing: LLMs Recognize Ambiguity but Rarely Ask Clarifying Questions

## Abstract
User queries are often underspecified and may admit multiple valid interpretations. Rather than silently making assumptions about the user's intent, a helpful assistant should surface such ambiguity by asking a clarifying question. Doing so requires two abilities: recognizing that a query is ambiguous, and acting on that recognition by seeking clarification instead of answering directly. To study these abilities, we evaluate models on ambiguous, unambiguous, and disambiguated questions in three settings: standard question answering, explicit ambiguity judgment, and behavioral analysis, where a judge model classifies responses as direct answers, refusals, or clarifying questions. We find a clear gap between recognition and behavior: models often identify ambiguity when explicitly asked to judge it, yet in the QA setting they overwhelmingly default to direct answers. Retrieved context further widens this gap by improving answerability while making models even less likely to ask clarifying questions.

## Methods
We study these questions on AmbigQA (Min et al., 2020), a dataset containing ambiguous questions, unambiguous questions, and disambiguated rewrites of the ambiguous ones. We conduct three complementary evaluations: (1) QA accuracy on ambiguous, unambiguous, and disambiguated questions, both with and without retrieved context; (2) ambiguity awareness by asking models to decide whether each question is ambiguous, with and without context; (3) clarification behavior, examining for each question how often the model asks a clarifying question, directly answers, or explicitly refuses and indicates more information is needed.

**Dataset.** AmbigQA is built from the open-domain version of Natural Questions and identifies all possible answers to an open-domain question along with disambiguated questions. Each example has a question, one or more answer strings, and a set of retrieved Wikipedia passages (context). The dataset contains: ambiguous questions (admitting multiple plausible interpretations, annotated with multiple disambiguated question–answer pairs), unambiguous questions (single clear interpretation), and disambiguated rewrites (each ambiguous question rewritten into several more specific questions, each with its own answer). We randomly sample 1,000 items: 425 unambiguous and 575 ambiguous. The 575 ambiguous items disambiguate into 2,460 sub-questions (~4.3 per ambiguous question).

**Task Formulation.** Each model is evaluated in two explicit settings plus a behavioral analysis:
- *Question Answering*: the model is prompted with the original question, with or without retrieved context, and answers directly; correctness is measured against reference answers.
- *Ambiguity Judgment*: the model determines whether a question is ambiguous or unambiguous (binary classification task); this probes explicit ambiguity awareness.
- *Behavioral Analysis*: model outputs in both settings are classified as a direct answer, a clarifying question, or an explicit refusal/indication that more information is needed. In the ambiguity-judgment setting, the ambiguity categories the model assigns are analyzed and compared to human annotations, using a manually defined taxonomy of six ambiguity types (Temporal, Identity, Version, Scope, Semantic, Locale) plus an "Other" category.

**Models and Metrics.** 10 models across 3 families: 6 from OpenAI (GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o-Mini, GPT-4.1-Mini), 2 from Claude (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Claude-3.5-Haiku), and 2 from Qwen (Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-7B). Accuracy is the main reported metric; F1, precision, and recall are reported in the Appendix. A judge model (gpt-5-nano) classifies QA responses into refusal / clarifying question / answered / only-answered categories (categories are not mutually exclusive). A separate judge model (gpt-5) maps ambiguous questions and model explanations to ambiguity-taxonomy categories.

## Results

**QA setting.** Ambiguous queries are harder to answer correctly than unambiguous ones for every model, with or without context (roughly a 10–15 point accuracy drop for stronger models). Retrieved context amplifies this gap: e.g., for GPT-4o-mini the unambiguous–ambiguous performance gap is 5.4% without context but 16.4% with context; a similar trend holds for Claude and Qwen. Adding retrieved context boosts QA accuracy overall — unambiguous questions go from 54% to 67% on average, ambiguous questions from 46% to 55%. Smaller/weaker models (Qwen 7B/14B, Haiku, minis) gain 15–30 points with context, while frontier models gain a more modest 2–8 points. Even with retrieval, ambiguous questions lag: top models reach 70%+ on unambiguous queries with context but only ~60% on ambiguous queries with context. Ambiguous queries gain more from context in relative terms, possibly because unambiguous questions benefit more from extra passages while ambiguous questions remain limited by missing intent rather than missing knowledge. Model ranking is relatively stable across conditions: GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, and Claude Sonnet are consistently near the top, Qwen-2.5-7B at the bottom.

**Ambiguity-judgment setting.** Most models achieve reasonably high accuracy on the ambiguous class (often 60–80%) but are noticeably worse at recognizing unambiguous questions, especially without context — suggesting a bias toward over-predicting ambiguity. Retrieved context does not improve recognition of ambiguous questions; instead its main effect is to improve performance on the unambiguous class, while accuracy on ambiguous questions stays flat or even declines for some models. This suggests models are not reliably using retrieved passages to reason about whether a query still admits multiple interpretations — the presence of supporting passages seems to act mainly as a cue that the question is answerable, pushing models toward the unambiguous label. GPT-4 shows an extreme version of this pattern: ~90% accuracy on unambiguous questions but only 15–27% on ambiguous questions in both context conditions.

**Behavioral analysis.** Across all models and conditions, the dominant behavior is giving a direct answer: answer rates are typically above 95%, and 80–95% of responses are judged as "only answered" (answer with no refusal, no clarifying question). Refusals are almost never observed, and clarifying questions are similarly rare. This reinforces the "knowing but not showing" pattern: models can recognize ambiguity when explicitly asked to judge it, but in the QA setting they almost always commit to an answer instead. Providing context slightly increases the share of pure answers and further suppresses the already-tiny clarification rates. The Claude family is most likely to ask clarifying questions (up to ~5% on ambiguous questions without context), with Qwen2.5-14B also showing a small increase; but even for these models, adding context consistently reduces clarification rate. This suppression is stronger for unambiguous than for ambiguous questions — once context is present, most models almost never ask for clarification on unambiguous queries.

**Ambiguity-type analysis.** Restricting to ambiguous questions correctly labeled as ambiguous, and mapping model explanations to the six-category taxonomy via a judge model, Identity and (to a lesser extent) Version ambiguities are consistently over-assigned relative to humans across almost all models and context conditions, while Scope ambiguities are strongly under-assigned — humans frequently explain ambiguity in terms of missing scope information. GPT-4 amplifies this trend most strongly, with particularly large over-use of the Identity category.

## Conclusions
The results point to two main implications. First, there is a misalignment between current reward signals and model faithfulness: models often know a query is ambiguous, but default behavior is to act as if it were fully specified. This calls for improved reward functions and training procedures that explicitly incentivize models to reveal what they know — reward objectives that positively reward expressing uncertainty, asking clarifying questions, and acknowledging under-specification, rather than only encouraging answer accuracy.

Second, there is a striking asymmetry in how models use retrieved context: in the QA setting, context reliably improves performance, but in the ambiguity-judgment setting, the same context provides little benefit and can even make models less likely to flag questions as ambiguous. This suggests models rely on superficial signals when deciding if a query is ambiguous rather than reasoning over the given context; improving models' ability to reason over retrieved context is an important direction for enhancing ambiguity-awareness.

More broadly, when probing what LLMs "know" or estimating their capabilities, task formulation matters: the same model can display quite different levels of ambiguity awareness depending on whether it is prompted to answer a question or to analyze the question itself. Although the paper focuses on ambiguity awareness, the same reasoning applies to other model behaviors where truthfully expressing what the model knows is not perfectly aligned with reward.
