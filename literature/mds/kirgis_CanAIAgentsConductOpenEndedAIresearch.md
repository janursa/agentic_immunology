# Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies

## Abstract

Forecasts of explosive AI progress hinge on AI agents automating AI research. But evidence on whether agents can carry out open-ended AI research is thin. Current evaluations either test agents on narrow, verifiable tasks, which excludes open-ended research, or submit AI-generated papers to blind peer review, which is overstretched, stochastic, and suffers from poor review quality. We introduce a third way to measure progress towards AI R&D automation. An agent takes on the central, open-ended research question of a high-quality unpublished paper, and the paper's original authors grade its output. We call these shadow evaluations. We ran shadow evaluations on two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions, giving frontier agents six days and thousands of dollars of compute. The agents completed all of the engineering without human help, yet could not make substantial progress towards answering the research questions. As a result, both papers were unambiguously rejected by the authors. We identify five recurring failure modes: poor judgment about the bar for publishable research, uncreative responses to shortcomings in the research design, ineffective backtracking from dead ends, poor resource awareness, and instruction drift. A robustness check with a second model and scaffold reproduced these failures. We release the expert reviews, survey responses, agent repositories, and logs. Our results provide early evidence that today's agents can do the engineering of AI research, but struggle with critical parts of the research lifecycle.

## Methods

**Shadow evaluations.** We develop a new method for measuring progress towards automating AI research: taking the central research question from a research paper that is not yet public, giving it to a well-resourced frontier agent, and asking the paper's original authors to review the agent's output as they would a conference submission. The agent "shadows" the original study — it works on the same research question as the original authors, without access to their paper or findings. Because the findings are not on the web or in the agent's training data, the evaluation is uncontaminated; because the authors spent months on the same question, they can judge in detail whether the agent made progress, which blind reviewers might lack the expertise to do. Shadow evaluations are also repeatable, since new unpublished papers with willing authors can serve as new test cases. Limitations of this design include non-blind reviewing (reviewers already answered the question themselves and know the paper is AI-generated), a small sample size (in-depth grading requires experts and days of review), and the judgment required at every step (selecting papers, designing the scaffold, interpreting logs).

**Task and research setup.** We partnered with the authors of two papers submitted to NeurIPS 2026 that were not yet public. The first ("Personas") concerns the structure and controllability of LLM personas, with coauthors David Africa and Konstantinos Voudouris (UK AI Security Institute) helping set up the experiment and review the submission (paper since made public: Baines et al., 2026). The second ("TabPFN") concerns designing a distribution-shift detector for tabular foundation models, in collaboration with Viet Nguyen (University of Toronto). Full research questions are given in Appendix A of the source paper.

The original authors were involved at three stages: formulating the research question for the agent without hinting at promising paths; helping set resource budgets sufficient to substantively address the question; and grading the finished papers as conference reviewers.

Both experiments ran Claude Opus 4.8 with extra-high reasoning on OpenClaw (a model-provider-agnostic scaffold), with dry runs against OpenAI and Anthropic models beforehand. Agents had full access to a Linux VM on AWS, could monitor their own API spend, compute budget, and remaining time, could delegate work to subagents, and kept a running research log. Agents had access to a self-review subagent that saw only the finished PDF and a NeurIPS review template, and were instructed to use three external AI reviewing tools: the Stanford Agentic Reviewer, the CMU Paper Reviewer, and refine.ink (one paid credit, $60, via API). Guidance given to the agents was not specific to either research question; scaffold modifications were limited to changes applicable to ML research generally, and every human intervention was documented.

Three human interventions were required: (1) a scaffold bug fix for an OpenClaw issue affecting Anthropic reasoning models; (2) a 24-hour deadline extension after agents submitted drafts self-graded as "Weak Reject," given to elicit upper-bound performance; (3) a request to rewrite inscrutable initial drafts for accessibility.

Resource budgets: Personas run used $1,130 of $3,000 in Anthropic API credit and $392 of $500 GPU compute; TabPFN run used $1,235 of $3,000 API credit and $69 of $100 GPU compute, both within a 120-hour deadline plus a 24-hour extension.

A pre-registered survey of twelve CRUX coauthors (working on AI research, evaluation, and AI policy) collected predictions before any results were shared, to characterize priors and disagreement; respondents were given full scaffold details but not the research questions.

**Robustness check.** To rule out "scaffold overhang" (a capability hindered by a broken tool or scaffold artifact rather than a genuine limitation), the TabPFN experiment was rerun using Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol at "ultra" reasoning level (a multi-agent orchestration analogous to the OpenClaw approach), under the same time and API budgets.

## Results

**No research at conference caliber.** Authors reviewed agent outputs on a 1–6 scale mirroring AI-conference reviews. Both papers were rejected: Personas scored 2 ("Reject"), TabPFN scored 1 ("Strong Reject") (full criterion scores: Quality 2/4 and 1/4; Clarity 1/4 and 2/4; Significance 2/4 and 2/4; Originality 3/4 and 2/4; Confidence 4/5 and 5/5). Both reviews cited poorly motivated data/experiments, no novel contribution, and impenetrable prose. Survey respondents had predicted a median 30% probability of a weak accept or better.

**Minor findings of interest.** Both authors were impressed by the literature reviews and by agents' use of hundreds of GPU-hours without issue, and noted candidate hypotheses resembled their own initial approaches. While the research questions were not substantively answered, agents produced minor findings authors found relevant (e.g., in Personas, narrow fine-tuning on misaligned *style* alone did not translate to broad misgeneralization, a counterintuitive result relative to typical emergent-misalignment findings). Respondents had predicted a median 60% chance of meeting this more moderate bar.

**Premature commitment to unpromising approaches.** Despite instructing agents to spend a minimum time on early-stage exploration (a 48-hour "gate," overridable by a subagent), both agents still developed reasonable hypotheses, then quickly rejected them using small, underpowered datasets, and always began with the most ambitious hypothesis before settling on the weakest. In Personas, the agent planned 36–48 hours across three methods but abandoned the other two after five hours of promising initial results on the first. 10 of 12 survey respondents had expected agents to take such shortcuts.

**Ineffective use of AI review feedback.** Self-review subagents worked as designed — across many revision rounds (four for Personas, ten for TabPFN) they never once returned an acceptance — but agents treated this as a cue to narrow claims and add caveats rather than rethink premises. Agents also mishandled disagreement across reviewers: the lenient Stanford Agentic Reviewer recommended acceptance on early drafts, while the stricter CMU Paper Reviewer, refine.ink, and self-review were far less optimistic ("the draft reads as a converted internal document," "the results hinge on n=1 cells"); agents overweighted the lenient signal in their final reports. Comparing agents' final self-reviews to human expert reviews found broad topical overlap but different prioritization — human-flagged critical issues (e.g., hand-curated/synthetic data selection) appeared in agent reviews but buried among many minor comments. This points to a possible generator–verifier gap: AI reviewers reliably rejected drafts, but since both final papers were in fact rejects, the reviewers' discernment accuracy could not be established. Half of survey respondents expected productive self-review; many others expected reviews to miss novelty assessment.

**Engineering competence.** Agents completed large literature reviews, debugged GPU environments, ran hundreds of experiments and robustness checks, retrieved external reviews via web/email, and compiled full camera-ready LaTeX documents, without manual intervention except logistical fixes (scaffold bug, credentials, extending the deadline, requesting a rewrite) and resolved all but one environmental barrier (a patched OpenClaw bug). 9 of 11 respondents had expected a loop of unresolvable errors — this expectation did not materialize.

**Presentation failures.** Both papers would have been desk-rejected at NeurIPS: content exceeded the nine-page limit. The Personas paper had zero visualizations in the main text versus 15 in the original authors' paper. Both agent papers had fewer references than the originals (36 vs. 69 for TabPFN; 16 vs. 52 for Personas). 7 of 11 respondents predicted obvious misformatting.

**No significant reward hacking.** Review of raw LLM calls and committed code found no evidence of agents hiding or misrepresenting experiments/data to support a more compelling conclusion; if anything, agents retired more marketable claims in favor of negative results. Agents produced reproducible code (a single script reproducing all results), though repositories were disorganized. Two other safety-relevant behaviors were noted: one agent committed an access token to its repository, and five instances of subagents hallucinating/misrepresenting results were caught and corrected by the orchestrator agent before reaching the final draft. Only 2 of 11 respondents expected catastrophic actions; 4 of 11 expected misreporting; 7 of 11 expected p-hacking/cherry-picking.

**Effect of reasoning effort.** Two earlier dry runs without reasoning (same papers, Opus 4.8) showed the same failure modes plus worse literature review and writing quality, producing inscrutable papers faster and well before the deadline. This suggests more reasoning effort per model call improved output quality, but more wall-clock time/resources likely would not change results; agents timed out at max reasoning in dry runs, so extra-high reasoning was used for final runs.

**Limited success using a frontier model to improve the harness.** After manual log analysis of the pilots, Claude Fable 5 (Claude Code, goal mode, "ultracode" setting) was tasked with editing the OpenClaw scaffold based on observed failures. The same failure modes recurred in this process: the model overweighted an n=1 sample, made broad, idiosyncratic scaffold changes that did not address the underlying problems.

**Five recurring failure modes (log analysis).** (1) *Lack of judgment about the bar for high-quality research*: agents used underpowered synthetic or hand-picked datasets and coalesced on a research direction before it was warranted; internal reviews were inflated relative to expert reviewers, mostly returning "Weak Reject" for papers experts unambiguously rejected. (2) *Lack of creative problem solving*: agents proposed reasonable, creative initial hypotheses (judged so by both expert authors) but, once falsified, responded to negative AI-review feedback with minor edits, caveats, and less ambitious hypotheses rather than reframing the question or redesigning experiments — producing thorough negative-result papers instead of new ideas ("grew narrower and less interesting as it discarded each one," per David Africa). This is linked to LLMs' known weakness at questioning a request's premise (e.g., unsaturated "trick question" benchmarks like SimpleBench) and corroborated by other evidence (frontier models failing to improve at a complex board game across 30 trials despite a scratchpad, per Epoch AI). (3) *Lack of effective backtracking*: agents backtracked locally (rerunning experiments, adding robustness checks) but never at the project level; despite consistently negative self-reviews, neither agent abandoned its approach and restarted — in TabPFN, the agent instead reframed the goal, arguing no such detector could exist, after falsifying six approaches within the first 14 hours (with 110 hours still remaining). This was not a scaffold limitation, since agents routinely spawned clean-context subagents for other purposes but rarely used them to restart. (4) *Lack of context awareness*: both runs ended with less than half the API budget spent; one agent declared completion seven hours before deadline after a reject verdict. This mirrors PostTrainBench's leaderboard note that GPT-5.5 had to be manually prompted to continue before its time budget expired — agents appear poorly calibrated to what they can accomplish per hour of wall-clock time. (5) *Instruction drift*: agents used the paid refine.ink review credit in only one of two runs, both final papers exceeded set length limits, and agents acknowledged but then ignored rules on exploration time, consistent with failure to manage context/instructions across a multi-day run.

**Robustness experiment (Codex + GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra, TabPFN rerun).** Many of the same failure modes recurred: underpowered experiments, no novel contribution, misformatted figures, no appendices. Budget mismanagement occurred but differently — GPT-5.6 exhausted the $3,000 API budget in just over two days (leaving ~100 hours unused), spending most of its budget iterating across hypotheses before scaling a candidate solution, then using only a couple of iterations to write up given a depleted budget. Positive findings also reproduced: AI self-review appropriately continued returning rejects, the agent registered hypotheses and reported a negative finding honestly rather than fabricating a positive one, and — unlike the OpenClaw runs, which used almost exclusively synthetically-shifted data — this run found and used a real-world distribution-shifted dataset.

## Conclusions

**Limitations.** Elicitation concerns: OpenClaw was chosen for provider-agnosticism, but an early pilot with GPT-5.3 Codex was abandoned when it couldn't use the scaffold effectively; the GPT-5.6/Codex robustness check received less harness-engineering effort than the main Opus 4.8/OpenClaw runs, which had multiple rounds of refinement. A crash-inducing conflict between OpenClaw's agent loop and Anthropic's thinking-block signatures required manual session resets (14 times in TabPFN, 5 in Personas); this did not appear to affect final paper quality or failure modes, since the two runs differed sharply in bug frequency but not in outcome. Two-thirds of survey respondents thought a failed run could be explained by scaffold limitations. On the six-day time budget: this exceeds most existing automated-research evaluations but is far less than the original authors spent; the authors argue this did not meaningfully affect results since (a) neither agent fully used its resources and expert objections centered on experiment/data judgment rather than quantity, and (b) compute budgets were set from authors' own estimates of what would suffice for one specific research question. The team could not test Anthropic's strongest model (Fable 5), as Anthropic deliberately limited it on frontier AI R&D. These findings contrast with related work: the same core team found an OpenClaw/Opus 4.6 agent could autonomously build and ship an iOS app, and found in other work that frontier models can now reproduce published research much better than two years ago — suggesting agents specifically struggle with certain open-ended research tasks, not engineering or verifiable-research tasks generally.

**Implications for accelerating AI research.** The paper offers preliminary evidence that AI agents are not yet capable of conducting open-ended autonomous AI research, though this may not resolve the broader debate on accelerating AI R&D — it is possible full automation of open-ended tasks isn't on the critical path to AI R&D acceleration. Still, the verifiable-task/open-ended-research distinction is argued to be germane: Anthropic's "When AI Builds Itself" post cites rising success on LLM-judged "open-ended" Claude Code sessions as evidence of self-improvement, and a report from the Elasticity Institute on the economics of recursive self-improvement distinguishes "broad" from "narrow" capabilities and calls for more data comparing AI and human researchers across the full capability range — the authors intend their experiments as early evidence toward that question.

**Potential biases.** Open-world evaluations involve substantial researcher discretion in question selection, study design, and execution, so the core team's prior beliefs (including skepticism of imminent runaway recursive self-improvement) could shape both the evaluation design and interpretation of results — e.g., framing the failures as lack of creativity/judgment is one interpretation among several coauthors raised (failures of reasoning/logic, epistemic lock-in). The authors argue there is no fully "unbiased" way to run such evaluations (unlike narrower, more objective benchmarks) and propose that open-world evaluations are most persuasive when conducted by epistemically diverse teams that disclose biases and surface disagreements explicitly — steps taken here include multiple scaffold-focused dry runs, generous agent budgets, and log analysis targeting known failure modes such as insufficient exploration. Unknown biases remain possible, particularly in log-analysis-based failure identification; the team releases full expert reviews, survey responses, agent repositories, and run logs so readers can independently check the interpretation. The authors plan to continue evaluating AI R&D automation on new research and welcome adversarial collaborations for follow-up studies.
